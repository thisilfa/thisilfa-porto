# Design Benchmark

Research date: 15 November 2026  
Purpose: identify transferable principles for a credible, distinctive AI/ML engineering portfolio. References are not templates to copy. Observations describe the live pages available on the research date; visual conclusions that go beyond page text are marked as inferences.

## Benchmark frame

The benchmark uses three groups:

1. **Product-standard references** for hierarchy, technical evidence, and system consistency.
2. **Authored portfolio references** for personal identity and memorable interaction.
3. **Category peers** for recruiter flow, project proof, and common failure modes in AI-engineer portfolios.

The target is not to imitate a SaaS homepage or turn the portfolio into a game. It is to combine product-grade clarity, authored personality, and evidence that a technical reviewer can trust.

## Reference analysis

### 1. Figma — product clarity at large content scale

URL: <https://www.figma.com/>

Observed:

- The current page leads with one broad product proposition, then gives distinct jobs such as designing, building, and shipping their own named sections.
- Product UI and concrete tool imagery carry the argument; copy does not have to describe every capability abstractly.
- Section headings name an action or product responsibility rather than generic categories.

Transferable principle:

- Give each portfolio section one job and one proof object. A project visual should show the system’s distinctive behavior, not repeat a brand ornament.

Do not copy:

- Figma’s product breadth and high visual density would overwhelm a personal portfolio if reproduced literally.

### 2. Linear — evidence embedded in a disciplined system

URL: <https://linear.app/>

Observed:

- The proposition is followed immediately by a realistic product surface containing issue states, labels, activity, and workflow detail.
- Long pages maintain coherence through repeated figure language, strict alignment, restrained color, and clear transitions between claim and interface proof.
- Technical language is specific but readable: planning, agents, issues, reviews, and integrations.

Transferable principle:

- Let sanitized system artifacts behave like figures in an engineering document. Use stable captions, consistent frames, and explicit links between a decision and its operational effect.

Do not copy:

- Dark gradients, glass panels, and product-dashboard aesthetics are already common in developer portfolios and would obscure Silfaradila’s warmer identity.

### 3. Anthropic — editorial authority for technical subject matter

URL: <https://www.anthropic.com/>

Observed:

- A direct mission statement leads; content is then organized through research, policy, commitments, products, and model documentation.
- Product announcements are paired with model details and system-card links, so claims can be inspected at greater depth.
- The page reads more like an institution’s publication than a conventional startup feature grid.

Transferable principle:

- Pair every high-level claim with a path to method, status, or limitation. Editorial restraint can communicate technical seriousness without adopting a cold interface.

Do not copy:

- Institutional scale and mission language are inappropriate for an individual engineer.

### 4. xAI — fast claim-to-demo progression

URL: <https://x.ai/>

Observed:

- The hero states a narrow technical category and routes users to API access or documentation.
- The next layer exposes concrete modalities, example prompts, code, model families, latency, and API activity.
- Developers receive a direct implementation surface rather than only marketing descriptions.

Transferable principle:

- A technical portfolio should move quickly from positioning to a tangible system example: architecture, workflow, output, metric, or code-adjacent artifact.

Do not copy:

- Large uncontextualized metrics or “frontier” language. Silfaradila’s figures must remain tied to their actual evaluation scope.

### 5. Rauno Freiberg — a personal identity built through editing

URL: <https://rauno.me/>

Observed:

- The page is extremely concise: identity, selected bodies of work, archive links, and a short personal operating credo.
- The language is authored and memorable without a long biography.
- Navigation names real bodies of work rather than generic skill categories.

Transferable principle:

- Distinction can come from what is omitted. A smaller number of strong, well-framed artifacts gives more authority than an exhaustive skills catalogue.

Do not copy:

- The radical brevity would not give recruiters enough context for Silfaradila’s confidential and partially attributable professional systems.

### 6. Bruno Simon — the portfolio itself as proof

URL: <https://bruno-simon.com/>

Observed:

- The portfolio is an explorable 3D world with explicit controls, quality settings, accessibility paths, and technical “behind the scene” documentation.
- The interaction is inseparable from the author’s specialty as a creative developer.
- The site offers fallbacks and tells visitors how to recover if they get stuck.

Transferable principle:

- A signature interaction is valuable only when it demonstrates the person’s actual discipline. For this portfolio, a responsive system map or evaluation explorer would be more authentic than decorative 3D.

Do not copy:

- Game navigation, WebGL spectacle, audio, or interaction that delays access to case-study evidence.

### 7. AviosIT — category clarity with an inventory problem

URL: <https://aviosit.com/>

Observed:

- The site clearly names the desired roles and offers Projects and CV actions immediately.
- It provides a structured engineering flow and extensive capability categories.
- The home page lists many tools and credentials.

Transferable principle:

- Role clarity and recruiter actions should remain obvious above the fold.

Warning:

- Long technology inventories quickly become interchangeable with other AI portfolios. Silfaradila should show fewer capabilities through project decisions rather than more labels.

### 8. Mark Spencer Jordan — proof-led project cards, with metric risk

URL: <https://www.markspencerjordan.com/>

Observed:

- Featured projects contain a separate “Proof” line, date/category labels, stack, and case-study link.
- The portfolio makes availability and desired project types explicit.
- A voice agent demonstrates an area of specialization directly in the portfolio.

Transferable principle:

- Separate the project claim, evidence, and stack so a recruiter can scan each independently.

Warning:

- Metric walls and self-scored skill bars can look authoritative without showing evaluation context. Silfaradila’s existing caution around metrics is a competitive strength and should be preserved.

### 9. Youssef Ibrahim — recruiter-specific interpretation, with density risk

URL: <https://yibrahim.com/>

Observed:

- Work, research, and a source-linked portfolio copilot are distinct destinations.
- The page foregrounds current project signal and explains why evidence matters to a hiring reader.
- The interface offers role lenses and recruiter summaries.

Transferable principle:

- Explain the relevance of a system, not only its technology. A reviewer should know what judgment the project demonstrates.

Warning:

- A very large quantity of content and fit scores can feel self-evaluated and create a scanning burden. The proposed redesign should not add a recruiter dashboard or invented fit scores.

### 10. Lulo / Yesid portfolio case study — recruiter-friendly structure

URL: <https://www.luloai.com/case-studies/yesid-portfolio>

Observed:

- The case study identifies curation, domain categories, technology tags, résumé access, concise positioning, responsive minimalism, and direct actions as the key portfolio mechanics.
- It explicitly recommends project summaries containing role, stack, and measurable impact.

Transferable principle:

- Keep the hiring path short and the project taxonomy legible.

Warning:

- Technology tagging is useful metadata, not a substitute for showing architecture, constraints, and evaluation.

## Category patterns to avoid

The peer review exposed several common AI-portfolio conventions that should not drive this redesign:

- Dark navy or black surfaces with violet/cyan gradients as an automatic signal for “AI.”
- Glowing neural networks, particle fields, circuit-board backgrounds, and floating model logos.
- Skill percentage bars or self-assigned proficiency scores.
- Uncontextualized “accuracy,” “latency,” or “production-grade” claims.
- Chatbots or voice agents added only to prove that an API can be connected.
- Nine equal project cards with identical screenshot placeholders.
- Dense badge clouds that force the reader to infer what was actually built.
- Case studies that hide role, status, limitations, or evidence provenance.

## Extracted design principles

1. **Evidence before inventory.** Show three strong systems in depth; compress the rest into a credible archive.
2. **One section, one argument.** Give every screen a named purpose and a visual proof object.
3. **Project-specific visuals.** Architecture, process, evaluation, and output should differ from project to project.
4. **Claim, scope, evidence.** Present these as separate readable layers.
5. **Editorial restraint, technical precision.** Warmth is compatible with engineering rigor.
6. **Authorship through selection.** Do not display every technology at equal importance.
7. **Mobile comprehension, not desktop shrinkage.** Convert system maps to vertical flows and compact archives.
8. **A signature moment must be relevant.** Prefer a system/evaluation interaction over ambient AI decoration.
9. **Bilingual parity by meaning.** Preserve technical truth and route equivalence without forcing identical line lengths.
10. **No invented proof.** A labeled absence or limitation is more credible than a fabricated screenshot or metric.

## Opportunity statement

There is room for a portfolio that feels like a well-edited engineering dossier rather than a developer template or AI startup landing page. Silfaradila already owns the hardest part: careful attribution and honest evaluation boundaries. The design opportunity is to make that rigor visible within seconds, using project-specific system evidence and a consistent editorial-technical composition.
